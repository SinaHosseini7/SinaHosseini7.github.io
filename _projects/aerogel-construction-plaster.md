## Motivation

In my BSc thesis, I asked whether silica aerogel added to construction gypsum could lower the plaster's heat-transfer coefficient and so reduce heat loss through building walls. Aerogel is a strong insulator, but a wall plaster also has to set, hold together, and carry load. For that reason, I tested mechanical behavior and density alongside heat transfer.

## Approach

I used silica-aerogel granules: one or two batches that I synthesized, and commercial aerogel for the rest. The granules were hydrophobic, so I used a surfactant to make them slightly hydrophilic before mixing them into the wet gypsum.

Mixing was the main practical problem. The granules are very light and repel water, and they kept floating to the surface. I mixed by hand and placed the mixing container in ice water. The lower temperature slowed the setting of the plaster and gave me more time to work. The best moment for stirring came near the end, when the paste became as thick as honey.

I made about 25 samples, with aerogel contents from 1 to 50 % by volume. Their shape and size depended on the test, and I could prepare up to five samples in a full day. I also cast one sample with 75 % aerogel by volume, but it was too fragile to test. I measured three properties:

- **Heat-transfer coefficient**, with a Hilton H112A heat conduction unit, on coin-shaped samples 0.5 to 5 mm thick.
- **Compressive behavior**, with a hydraulic press in a laboratory of the Civil Engineering Department. My main aim was the breaking point, and I also used the load data to approximate Young's modulus.
- **Density**, by the Archimedes method.

## Results

The measured data belong to the University of Zanjan, so I do not report values on this page. What the project gave me is a working method: a way to mix a light, hydrophobic filler into a setting binder, and a set of thermal and mechanical tests that each needed its own sample geometry.
