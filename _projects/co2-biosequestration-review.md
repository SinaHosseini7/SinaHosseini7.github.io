## Motivation

Chemical CO₂ capture with amines works, but the solvent needs heat to regenerate, corrodes equipment, and degrades over time. Carbonic anhydrase (CA) is an enzyme that speeds up the conversion of CO₂ to bicarbonate in water, without secondary pollutants. As a BSc student, I went to Dr. Mahdi Maleki Kakelar's office and asked to work with him. This review was the result.

## Approach

The review first compares physical, chemical, and biological routes to CO₂ capture. It then covers the structure and reaction mechanism of CA, the main methods for immobilizing the enzyme, and the challenges of industrial use. I read more than 120 sources for the review, and 63 of them are cited in the final paper.

## Results

The review's main findings are:

- CA is a zinc-containing enzyme and one of the fastest known, with turnover numbers of about 10⁴ to 10⁶ s⁻¹. It occurs in several classes, such as α, β, γ, and δ, which differ in structure and stability.
- Free CA is hard to use in industry. It cannot be recovered and reused, it is costly, and harsh process conditions reduce its activity.
- Immobilization addresses these problems. The review compares adsorption, covalent bonding and cross-linking, encapsulation, and entrapment. Adsorption is cheap and simple, but the weak bond lets the enzyme detach. Covalent bonding holds it more firmly: in one study, human CA bound to SBA-15 silica could be reused for 40 cycles.
- The main open challenges are mass-transfer limits in bioreactors, scale-up from the laboratory, and long-term stability under the changing temperature, pressure, and gas flow of real plants.
- Beyond capture, CA could also turn CO₂ into bicarbonate as a value-added product.
